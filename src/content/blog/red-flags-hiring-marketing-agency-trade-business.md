The clearest red flag when hiring a marketing agency is a guarantee of specific rankings or lead volume, because nobody can honestly promise either. After that: reporting built on clicks and impressions rather than booked jobs, long contracts with no exit, no experience in the trades, and vagueness about who actually does the work. Most agencies that fail home service businesses fail in the same handful of ways, and all of them are visible before you sign if you ask the right questions.

## Red flag 1: Guaranteed rankings or guaranteed leads

Nobody controls Google's results. An agency promising "page one in 90 days" is either inexperienced or counting on you not checking which keyword they ranked you for — often a phrase nobody searches, like your own business name plus a city.

Guaranteed lead volume is a softer version of the same problem. It is achievable if the definition of "lead" is loose enough. A form fill from someone outside your service area is a lead. A wrong number that came through a tracked line is a lead.

**What to ask instead:** "What would you expect to change in the first 90 days, and how would we know?" A good answer talks about specific, checkable things — profile fixes, pages built, response times — not positions.

## Red flag 2: Reporting on clicks and impressions

Impressions are not revenue. Clicks are not revenue. A monthly report full of graphs trending up while your calendar stays empty is the most common way contractors get quietly overcharged. This matters most with [Google Ads](/services/google-ads), where the platform hands over impressive-looking numbers by default.

The problem is structural rather than dishonest: it is easy to report what the ad platform gives you and hard to report on booked jobs, which requires connecting to your actual business.

**What to ask instead:** "How will we know whether this produced booked jobs?" If the answer stays at the level of website traffic, the reporting will too. For a home service business most conversions are phone calls, so call tracking tied back to the keyword is table stakes, not an upgrade.

## Red flag 3: No experience with home services

Marketing a trade business is not the same as marketing an ecommerce brand. The differences are structural:

- Most conversions are phone calls, not form fills
- Urgency dominates — the business that answers first often wins
- Geography caps the market, so scale comes from conversion rather than reach
- Seasonality swings demand enormously
- The [map pack](/services/local-maps) matters more than organic rankings

An agency that has never worked with contractors will optimize for form fills, run ads at hours nobody can answer, and report on metrics that do not reflect how the business actually earns.

**What to ask instead:** "Which trades have you worked with, and what did you learn that surprised you?" The second half is the useful part. Anyone can name clients.

## Red flag 4: Long contracts with no way out

Twelve-month minimums with no performance clause and no early exit are designed to protect the agency, not to give the work time to compound.

There is a legitimate version of the argument — SEO genuinely takes months, and an agency that rebuilds your site in month one deserves protection against you leaving in month two. The distinction is whether the lock-in is mutual. A contract that commits them to nothing while committing you to a year is not about giving the work time.

**What to ask instead:** "What happens if I want to leave in month four?" And separately: "Who owns the website, the ad account and the phone numbers if we part ways?" That second question surfaces the real risk.

## Red flag 5: You do not own your own assets

This one costs the most and gets noticed the latest.

Some agencies build your website on their platform, run ads from their own account, and route calls through numbers they own. Everything works until you leave, at which point you discover the site cannot be transferred, the ad account's history — and its optimization data — stays with them, and the number on your trucks belongs to someone else.

**What to ask instead:** "Will the Google Ads account be under my own billing, and can I have admin access to it?" The answer should be an immediate yes.

## Red flag 6: Vague about who does the work

"Our team of experts" can mean an experienced strategist or a rotating cast of offshore subcontractors who have never seen a furnace. Neither is disqualifying on its own, but you should know which you are buying.

The related warning sign is an agency that will not tell you what they will actually do this month. "Ongoing SEO optimization" is not a deliverable. It is a line item.

**What to ask instead:** "Walk me through what you would actually do in the first 30 days." Specificity is the signal. Vagueness here almost always means vagueness in the work.

## Red flag 7: They never tell you no

An agency that agrees with everything you suggest is selling compliance, not expertise.

If you say you want TikTok ads for a commercial plumbing business and they say "great idea," you are not getting advice. A good provider will sometimes tell you the thing you want is the wrong priority — that fixing your [missed calls](/services/missed-call-text-back) matters more than a new website, or that you should not scale ad spend until response is handled.

That last one is worth dwelling on. Paying for a click and then missing the resulting call is the most expensive mistake available, and an agency happy to increase your ad budget while the leak stays open is not looking after you.

**What to ask instead:** "What would you tell me not to spend money on right now?" A useful answer exists. Silence is informative.

## What should you check before signing?

Five things, none of which take long:

**Ask for two references in the trades** and actually call them. Ask what went wrong, not what went well — every relationship has friction, and how it was handled tells you more than a testimonial.

**Ask to see a real client report.** Anonymized is fine. You are checking whether it reports on jobs or on traffic.

**Confirm asset ownership in writing.** Website, ad accounts, phone numbers, domain.

**Understand the exit terms** before you need them.

**Check their own marketing.** An agency whose own site is slow, thin, or last updated three years ago is telling you something.

## What does a reasonable engagement look like?

Clear scope for the first 90 days with specific deliverables. Reporting that connects to booked work rather than website traffic. Assets in your name. An exit path that does not require a lawyer. And someone who will occasionally tell you that the thing you asked for is not the thing you need.

None of that guarantees results — nothing does. It does mean that if it is not working, you will be able to tell, and you will be able to leave.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should a contractor pay for marketing?

It varies far too much by market, trade and scope for a number to be meaningful, and anyone quoting one without knowing your situation is guessing. What matters more is whether the spend is tied to something checkable. A smaller budget producing tracked booked jobs beats a larger one producing reports about impressions.

### Is a 12-month contract always a red flag?

No. SEO genuinely takes months, and an agency doing substantial upfront work reasonably wants protection. The question is whether the commitment is mutual and whether there is any performance clause at all. A year-long lock-in with no obligations on their side is the problem, not the length itself.

### Should I hire a local agency or a specialist?

Specialization in home services matters more than being local. An agency across the country that understands how contractors actually earn will outperform a local generalist who optimizes for form fills. Local helps for face-to-face relationship, which some owners value highly — just do not trade away the specialization for it.

### What if my current agency is not producing results?

Ask for a report showing booked jobs attributable to their work. If they cannot produce one, that is the answer. Before leaving, secure access to your ad accounts, your website and your phone numbers — do that while the relationship is still cordial.

### Do I need an agency at all?

Not necessarily. A contractor who answers every call, [asks every customer for a review](/blog/automated-review-generation-home-service-business) and keeps their Google Business Profile current is doing most of what matters. Agencies earn their place when the volume or the complexity exceeds what the owner can personally run.

### What should an agency do in the first month?

Concrete, checkable things: audit and fix the Google Business Profile, set up call tracking and conversion measurement, identify where leads are leaking, and produce a plan with specific deliverables. If month one is "strategy and discovery" with nothing shipped, ask what you are paying for.
