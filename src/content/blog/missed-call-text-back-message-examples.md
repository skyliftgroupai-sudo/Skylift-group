A missed call text back should name your business, acknowledge the missed call directly, ask one specific question, and stop. Something like: "Hi, this is Dave at Miller Plumbing — sorry we missed your call. What do you need help with? I can get you on the schedule today." That works because it identifies you before they wonder who is texting, it explains why the message arrived, and it asks something they can answer with one thumb. Most missed-call texts fail because they are too long, too generic, or do not make clear which company is writing.

## Why does the wording matter so much?

Because the message arrives in a hostile context. The recipient just called a business, got no answer, and is now looking at a text from a number they do not recognize.

They will decide whether to engage in about two seconds. In that window the message has to answer three questions: who is this, why are they texting me, and what do they want. Get any of those wrong and it reads as spam.

The second constraint is that you are competing with time. They are still working down the search results. A message that requires thought, or that asks them to click something, loses to the competitor who is answering their phone.

Short, identified, and answerable. Everything else is optimization.

## What should the message include?

Five elements, in roughly this order.

**Your business name, early.** First few words. Not the last line. The recipient needs to know who this is before they decide whether to keep reading.

**A person's name, if you can.** "This is Dave at Miller Plumbing" outperforms "Miller Plumbing here." It reads as a human, which is the impression you want even though a system sent it.

**An acknowledgment of the missed call.** "Sorry we missed your call" explains why the message exists. Without it the text is unsolicited; with it, it is a response.

**One question.** Not three. "What do you need help with?" or "What's going on?" — something open enough to get the details, simple enough to answer immediately.

**A hint at what happens next.** "I can get you on the schedule" tells them replying leads somewhere. Without it, they may not see the point of answering.

## What should it leave out?

**Links.** A text from an unknown number containing a link looks like phishing, and some carriers filter messages containing links more aggressively. Save the link for later in the conversation, once they have replied.

**Pricing.** You do not know what the job is yet. Quoting anything at this stage either scares them off or commits you to a number you will have to walk back.

**Hours and policies.** "Our office is open Monday to Friday 8-5" is information they did not ask for and it implicitly tells them to go away.

**Multiple questions.** Each additional question lowers the chance of a reply. Get one answer, then have a conversation.

**Anything longer than about two lines.** Nobody reads a paragraph from a business they have not spoken to yet.

**Emoji and exclamation marks.** They read as marketing, which is the opposite of the impression you want when someone has an urgent problem.

## What do good messages look like?

Adapt these rather than copying them — the point is that it should sound like your business.

**General home service:**
"Hi, this is Dave at Miller Plumbing — sorry we missed your call. What do you need help with? I can get you on the schedule today."

**Emergency-heavy trades:**
"This is Sarah at Ace HVAC, sorry we missed you. Is this an emergency or can it wait until tomorrow? Either way I'll get someone out."

The second question is doing real work there — it triages while it engages.

**After hours:**
"Hi, this is Mike at Northside Roofing. We missed your call — it's after hours but I'm still reachable by text. What's the problem?"

Naming the hour explains the situation honestly and still keeps the conversation open, which beats an automated "we are closed."

**Quote-driven work:**
"This is Tom at Clearway Junk Removal, sorry we missed your call. What are you looking to get rid of? I can give you a rough price by text."

Here the next step is a price, so saying so gives them a reason to reply.

## Should the follow-up be different?

Yes, and most businesses skip the follow-up entirely, which leaves recoverable jobs on the table.

If the first message gets no reply, one follow-up a few hours later is reasonable. Not three, and not in five minutes.

The follow-up should be shorter and should not repeat the first message:
"Hi, Dave from Miller Plumbing again — still happy to help if you need us. Just reply here."

If that gets nothing, stop. A third message converts almost nobody and generates complaints, and complaints affect your carrier standing.

## How do I know if the message is working?

Track reply rate, not delivery rate.

Delivery tells you the message arrived. Reply rate tells you whether the wording works. If messages are going out and almost nobody responds, the problem is nearly always one of three things:

**They cannot tell who it is from.** Business name is too late in the message, or missing.

**It reads as automated.** Too formal, too long, or too obviously a template.

**It arrived too late.** If the delay is more than a couple of minutes, the caller has already moved on. This is usually a configuration issue rather than a wording one, and it is worth checking before rewriting anything — [the window that decides who gets the job](/blog/what-missed-calls-cost-contractors) is short.

Change one thing at a time. Rewriting the whole message and seeing a different result tells you nothing about which change mattered.

## Should the message change by time of day or job type?

Yes, and this is where most setups stop short.

A single message sent to every missed caller at every hour works, but it leaves value on the table. Two splits are worth making.

**By time.** During business hours the message can promise a quick callback, because someone will be there. After hours it should be honest about the time and lean harder on text as the channel. A message promising a callback "shortly" at 9pm sets an expectation you will not meet.

**By where the call came from.** If you run [Google Ads](/services/google-ads) with call tracking, you know which calls came from an ad and often which service they searched for. A caller who clicked an emergency plumbing ad can get a message that already assumes urgency, which saves a round trip.

The principle is that every piece of context you already hold should remove a question from the conversation. The fewer exchanges needed before booking, the more callers make it through.

## Do I need consent to send these?

A missed call text back replies to contact the customer initiated, which is a much stronger position than sending a promotion to someone who never contacted you. That is why it is usually the safest place to start with business texting.

You still need A2P 10DLC registration for the messages to reliably deliver, and you still need opt-out handling. And the moment your messaging moves beyond replying to inbound calls — seasonal offers, reactivation — [the consent question becomes central](/blog/text-customers-who-never-opted-in). This is not legal advice; confirm your obligations with your own counsel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How quickly should the text go out?

Within about a minute of the missed call. The window where you can still win the job is the few minutes while the caller is working down their search results. A reply fifteen minutes later often arrives after they have booked someone else.

### Should the text come from a person or the business?

Name a person and the business together — "this is Dave at Miller Plumbing." A named person reads as human and gets better reply rates than a company name alone, even when a system sent the message.

### Can I include a booking link?

Not in the first message. Links from unknown numbers look like phishing and are filtered more aggressively. Once the customer has replied and knows who you are, a link is fine.

### How many follow-ups should I send?

One, a few hours after the first if there was no reply. A third message converts almost nobody and risks complaints, which affect whether your messages get delivered at all.

### What if the caller replies with something the system cannot handle?

It should hand off to a person immediately with the conversation attached. Automated handling that loops a frustrated customer through the same response is worse than no automation.

### Should after-hours messages say different things?

Yes. Acknowledging the hour honestly while staying reachable by text beats an automated "we are closed," which tells an urgent customer to call someone else.
