Local SEO for a home service business comes down to three things in order of impact: a Google Business Profile configured correctly for the work you actually do, a steady flow of recent reviews, and service pages on your website that match what people search for. Everything else — blog volume, directory submissions, technical tinkering — is secondary and often a distraction. The reason most local SEO advice fails contractors is that it is written for businesses with a storefront and a national audience, not for a company that drives to customers inside one county.

## What is different about local SEO for contractors?

Three things, and they change the whole approach.

**You compete in a small geography.** A national ecommerce site competes with everyone. You compete with the six other HVAC companies within driving distance. That is a very different problem, and it is usually winnable.

**The map pack matters more than organic results.** Search for a plumber on a phone and count how much screen the map results take before you reach the blue links. Most people never scroll that far. This inverts the usual SEO priority — your [Google Business Profile](/services/local-maps) is more important than your website's ranking.

**Proximity is a ranking factor you cannot change.** The same business ranks first in one neighborhood and nowhere three miles away. No amount of optimization fully erases distance, which means part of local SEO is accepting where you can realistically win.

## What has the biggest impact on local rankings?

In the order worth working on:

### Your Google Business Profile primary category

More leverage than anything else, and the most commonly wrong. Google weights the primary category heavily when deciding what searches you are relevant for. An HVAC company categorized as "Air conditioning contractor" will struggle on heating searches regardless of how good the rest of the setup is.

Check what your top-ranking competitors use. If several share a category you do not, that is your first fix.

### Review recency and volume

Not just total count — recency. A profile with eighty reviews where the newest is over a year old reads as a business that has gone quiet, to Google and to customers alike. A competitor with thirty reviews, several from this month, looks active.

The fix is making review requests [automatic rather than remembered](/services/reviews), so some arrive every month without anyone deciding to ask.

### Service pages that match real searches

One page per service, genuinely written, not a paragraph under a heading. Someone searching "water heater replacement" should land on a page about water heater replacement, not a homepage listing eleven services.

This is where most contractor websites are weakest. A site with a services page listing everything in one place gives Google nothing specific to rank.

### Consistent business information

Name, address and phone identical everywhere — your site, your profile, directories, your Facebook page. Inconsistency dilutes the prominence signals that decide rankings. This is tedious and it genuinely works, which is rare.

## What is mostly a waste of time?

**Bulk directory submissions.** Submitting to two hundred low-quality directories does not build prominence. A handful of relevant, real citations does more.

**Blogging without a purpose.** Publishing weekly posts about general home maintenance does very little if none of them support a service page or answer a question a customer actually has. Volume is not the input; relevance is.

**Obsessing over technical scores.** A perfect score on some audit tool does not rank you. Genuinely broken things matter — pages that do not render, missing content, slow mobile load — but chasing a number past that point is displacement activity.

**Stuffing city names.** Pages listing forty towns you "serve" read as spam to both Google and humans. A real page about real work in a place you actually go beats forty thin ones.

**Copying competitors who break the rules.** Keyword-stuffed business names and fake locations do work until the listing gets suspended. [Report them instead](/blog/competitor-ranking-above-me-google-maps) — it costs nothing and risks nothing.

## Does my website still matter if the map pack dominates?

Yes, for two reasons.

The site your profile links to feeds relevance. A profile pointing at a homepage with no service detail gives Google less to work with than one pointing at a page specifically about the searched service.

And the map pack only shows three results. For every search where you are fourth, your organic listing is the only way you appear at all. The two work together rather than competing.

Practically: build a real page for each service you want to rank for, make it genuinely useful rather than a keyword container, and link to it from your navigation so Google can find it. A page reachable only from the sitemap tends not to get crawled at all.

## How long does local SEO take?

Profile fixes — categories, services, information consistency — can show effects within weeks. Review velocity and citation work compound over months. New service pages need to be crawled, indexed and then earn their position, which is a months-long process rather than a weeks-long one.

Anyone promising a specific position by a specific date is guessing. Google offers no such guarantee and neither should a provider. What is reasonable to expect is directional movement within a quarter if the fundamentals were genuinely broken and get genuinely fixed.

This is also why [Google Ads](/services/google-ads) and local SEO belong together rather than being alternatives. Ads produce calls while the organic side builds. The organic side keeps producing when you stop paying.

## What about AI search and answer engines?

Worth a short section, because it is changing and most advice has not caught up.

A growing share of people asking "who should I hire to replace a water heater in my area" get an answer from an AI assistant rather than a results page. Those systems mostly do not execute JavaScript, which means a website that renders its content in the browser is invisible to them — the page looks blank.

That is a technical problem with a technical fix, and it is worth checking: view your own site's page source and see whether your service copy is in there. If all you see is an empty container, AI crawlers see the same thing.

Beyond that, the things that make a page quotable are unglamorous. A direct answer near the top that stands on its own without the surrounding page. Clear headings phrased as the questions people actually ask. Specific, checkable statements rather than marketing adjectives. Content that says where something does not work, not just where it does.

None of this conflicts with ordinary local SEO — a page that answers a question clearly tends to rank well too. It is the same work with a second beneficiary.

## What should I do first?

If you do nothing else, do these three in this order:

1. **Fix your Google Business Profile primary category** and fill in your services individually. An afternoon of work with the largest single effect.
2. **Set up automatic review requests** after every completed job, so recency stops depending on memory.
3. **Build one real service page** for your highest-value service, and link it from your main navigation.

Then check your business information for consistency, and only then start thinking about content volume.

The order matters because the first three compound. Content published before the profile is right is content working against a handicap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does local SEO take to work for a contractor?

Profile and information fixes can show effects in weeks. Reviews and citations compound over months. New pages take months to rank. Expect directional movement within a quarter if the basics were broken, and treat any specific position guarantee as a red flag.

### Do I need a blog for local SEO?

Not for its own sake. A blog helps when the posts answer questions your customers actually ask and link to your service pages. Publishing general home maintenance content on a schedule does very little. Purpose first, volume second.

### Should I make a page for every town I serve?

Only for towns where you genuinely work and can write something real about. Forty thin pages each listing a town name read as spam. A handful of substantial service-area pages work. The test is whether the page would be useful to someone in that town.

### Is local SEO better than Google Ads?

They answer different questions. Ads produce calls immediately and stop when you stop paying. Local SEO takes months and keeps producing. Most contractors are best served running ads while the organic side builds, then keeping both.

### How many reviews do I need to rank in the map pack?

There is no threshold, and anyone quoting one is guessing. Recency and consistency matter more than a total. A system producing a few reviews every month beats a one-off push that produced twenty and then stopped.

### Does my website speed affect local rankings?

Genuinely broken performance hurts — pages that do not render on mobile, or take many seconds to load. Beyond that, chasing a higher score on an audit tool has far less effect than fixing your profile category or your review flow.
