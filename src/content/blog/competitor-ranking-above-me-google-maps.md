A competitor outranks you in the Google Map Pack for one of three reasons: their profile is more relevant to the search, they are closer to the person searching, or Google considers them more prominent. Quality of work is not a ranking factor — Google cannot assess it. In practice the most common cause for home service businesses is a primary category that does not match what people search for, followed by review recency and inconsistent business information across the web. All three are fixable; proximity is not.

## How does Google decide map pack rankings?

Google states three factors: relevance, distance and prominence.

**Relevance** is how well your profile matches what was searched. This is driven mostly by your primary category, your secondary categories, your listed services, and the content on the website you link to.

**Distance** is how far your business is from the searcher, or from the location in their search. You cannot change this, which is why the same business ranks first in one neighborhood and nowhere three miles away.

**Prominence** is how well known Google thinks you are. Reviews, citations across the web, links, and general online presence all feed it.

The useful consequence: two of the three are things you control. When a worse contractor outranks you, it is almost always because they have done better on relevance or prominence — or they are simply closer to whoever is searching.

## Is my primary category wrong?

Check this first. It has more leverage than anything else on the profile, and it is the most commonly wrong.

Google weights the primary category heavily when deciding what searches you are relevant for. An HVAC company listed primarily as "Air conditioning contractor" will struggle for heating searches. A plumber listed as "Plumber" competes differently than one listed as "Drainage service."

Look at what your top-ranking competitors use as their primary category. You can see this on their profile. If three of them share a category and you use a different one, that is worth a hard look.

Then add relevant secondary categories, but do not stuff them. Secondary categories carry far less weight, and a list of fifteen loosely related ones does not help.

Also fill in your services individually rather than leaving them implied. A profile that lists "furnace repair", "furnace installation", "AC repair" and so on gives Google explicit matches to work with.

## Are my reviews stale?

Review recency matters, not just total count.

A profile with eighty reviews where the newest is fourteen months old reads to Google — and to customers — as a business that has gone quiet. A competitor with thirty reviews, four of them this month, looks active.

This is the second most common gap we see. Contractors do a review push, get a batch, and then stop. The count looks healthy and the recency does not.

The fix is making review requests [automatic rather than remembered](/blog/automated-review-generation-home-service-business) — an [automated request by text](/services/reviews) after every completed job, so a few arrive every month without anyone deciding to ask.

Responding to reviews matters too. It is a visible activity signal, and prospective customers read your responses to critical reviews more carefully than they read the reviews themselves.

## Is my business information inconsistent?

Your name, address and phone number need to be identical everywhere they appear — your website, your Google profile, directories, trade associations, your Facebook page.

Inconsistency dilutes prominence. If Google finds three versions of your phone number and two spellings of your business name, it has less confidence about which is correct, and confidence is part of what prominence measures.

The usual culprits are old listings nobody remembers creating: a directory profile from five years ago with a disconnected number, a Facebook page with a previous address, an abbreviated company name on one site and the full legal name on another.

This is tedious to fix and genuinely moves the needle, which is an unusual combination in SEO.

## Is my service area set up correctly?

Home service businesses often get this wrong in a way that quietly limits them.

If you work from a location customers visit, you should have an address displayed. If you travel to customers, you should be configured as a service-area business with your address hidden. Mixing these up — or setting a service area that covers half the state when you realistically work one county — affects where you surface.

Setting an unrealistically large service area does not help you rank across it. It can dilute relevance for the area you actually serve.

## Is my competitor breaking the rules?

Sometimes, yes. The two common ones:

**Keywords stuffed into the business name.** "Smith Plumbing" becomes "Smith Plumbing | Emergency Plumber Near Me | Drain Cleaning." This violates Google's guidelines and it does affect rankings, which is why people do it.

**Fake or virtual locations.** Listings at addresses where no business operates, created to appear in more areas.

Both are reportable, and worth knowing about when [evaluating an agency](/blog/red-flags-hiring-marketing-agency-trade-business) that suggests you do the same. Google's redressal form exists for this, and reports from competitors are a normal part of how these get caught.

What you should not do is copy them. Suspension is the penalty, reversing one is slow and uncertain, and in the meantime you have no listing at all. Removing a competitor's unfair advantage costs you nothing and risks nothing. Copying it risks everything.

## What if they are just closer?

Then you may not be able to outrank them for searchers in that spot, and it is worth accepting that rather than burning budget on it.

Proximity is the one factor with no workaround. A business two miles from the searcher has an advantage over one twelve miles away, and no amount of optimization fully erases it.

What you can do is win the areas where you are closest, and compete on relevance and prominence everywhere else. That means service-area pages on your site that genuinely describe your work in those areas, reviews that mention the towns you serve, and the strongest possible profile — so that when you are second-closest, you are still the best match.

## How do I even know where I rank?

This trips up more contractors than it should, because map pack rankings are not a single position — they vary by where the searcher is standing.

Searching your own service from your office tells you almost nothing. Google knows where you are, it knows your search history, and you are physically at your own business, which is the single best position you could possibly rank from. The result you see is close to a best case.

A few things that give you a more honest picture:

**Search from different parts of your service area.** Literally, from a phone, in different towns. Tedious but real.

**Use an incognito window and set the location.** Chrome's developer tools let you override your location, which approximates searching from elsewhere without driving there.

**Look at grid-based rank tracking.** Tools exist that check your position from many points across a map and show the result as a heat map. This is the clearest picture available, and it usually reveals that you rank well within a couple of miles and drop off sharply beyond that.

The reason this matters: if you optimize based on what you see from your own desk, you will conclude you are doing fine while losing every search in the next town over. The grid view tends to change what people prioritize.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to move up in the map pack?

Category and information fixes can show effects within a few weeks. Review velocity and citation consistency work over months. Anyone promising a specific position by a specific date is guessing — Google gives no such guarantees and neither should a provider.

### Does my website affect my map pack ranking?

Yes, indirectly. The site your profile links to contributes to relevance, so a page that genuinely matches the search supports the listing above it. A profile pointing at a homepage with no service detail gives Google less to work with.

### Can I rank in a town where I have no address?

You can, but it is harder. Proximity favors businesses physically closer to the searcher. Relevance and prominence can partly offset it, which is where service-area pages, reviews mentioning those areas and a strong profile matter most.

### Should I report a competitor who stuffed keywords in their name?

Yes. It is a guideline violation, the redressal form exists for exactly this, and reports from competitors are routine. It costs you nothing and carries no risk.

### Do paid Google Ads help my organic map rankings?

No. Running ads does not improve your map pack position. They are separate systems. Ads can put you above the map pack while you work on ranking in it, which is a reason to run both, but one does not feed the other.

### How many categories should I add to my profile?

One primary, chosen carefully, plus the secondary categories that genuinely describe services you offer. Adding loosely related categories to cover more ground dilutes relevance rather than extending it.
