Text message marketing is worth it for most home service businesses, but not for the reason it usually gets sold. The value is not that texts get opened — it is that texting fits how homeowners actually behave when something breaks. They are at work, they cannot take a call, and they are contacting three companies at once. A text reaches them in that moment. An email does not, and a voicemail definitely does not. The businesses that get results from SMS are the ones using it for missed-call replies, appointment confirmations and review requests, not the ones blasting promotions to a purchased list.

## Why does texting work better than email for contractors?

Consider what is actually happening on the customer's end. Their water heater is leaking. They search, they call the first result, nobody picks up. They call the second. Somewhere in that sequence they are also at work, or driving, or holding a bucket.

Email assumes the recipient will sit down, open an inbox, and read. That is a reasonable assumption for a newsletter and a poor one for someone standing in a flooded utility room. Voicemail assumes they will listen to a message from an unknown number, which most people no longer do.

Texting fits the gap. It arrives on the device already in their hand, it can be read in three seconds, and it can be answered with one word while they are doing something else. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why texting outperforms in the trades specifically rather than in marketing generally.

There is a second reason that matters more than open rates: a text creates a written record. The appointment time, the address, the quoted range — all of it sits in a thread both sides can check. Phone bookings produce misheard addresses and disputed times. Texts do not.

## Where does SMS genuinely help a home service business?

A handful of specific uses work consistently. The rest mostly do not.

### Missed-call text back

The highest-value message by a wide margin. A call comes in while your crew is on a roof, goes unanswered, and within about a minute the caller gets a text asking what they need. The competitor they were about to dial never gets called.

This one message addresses the single most expensive failure in the trades: you paid to make the phone ring, and then nobody could answer it. If you set up nothing else, set up [missed call text back](/services/missed-call-text-back).

### Appointment confirmations and reminders

A confirmation when the job is booked, a reminder the day before. No-shows in home services are usually forgetfulness rather than intent, and a reminder on the device the customer is holding fixes most of them. It also gives them an easy way to reschedule instead of simply not being home when your tech arrives.

### Technician en route

A short message when your tech leaves the previous job. This removes the most common inbound call your office fields — "is he still coming?" — and reads as professional rather than automated.

### Review requests

Sent shortly after the work is done, while the customer still feels good about it, with a direct link. Asking by text works better than asking in person because the customer can act immediately rather than promising to do it later and forgetting. This is the mechanism behind [automated review generation](/services/reviews).

### Seasonal maintenance reminders

Furnace tune-ups before the first cold snap, AC checks before summer. These go to people you have already served, which makes them the cheapest work you will book all year. Run at scale, this becomes a [database reactivation campaign](/services/database-reactivation).

## Where does text marketing backfire?

Promotional blasts to people who never asked to hear from you. That is the short version, and it covers most of the ways businesses get this wrong.

A few specific failure modes:

**Texting a purchased or scraped list.** Beyond being a legal problem, this generates complaints fast, and complaints get your number filtered by carriers. Once that happens your appointment confirmations stop arriving too — you lose the transactional messages you actually depend on.

**Sending too often.** A text is more intrusive than an email, so the tolerance is lower. A message every week with a new discount trains people to opt out.

**Messages with no reason attached.** "Hi, just checking in!" is noise. Every message should be tied to something that happened — a call, a booking, a completed job, a season changing.

**Treating it as a broadcast channel.** Texting is a conversation. If customers reply and nobody answers for six hours, you have built something worse than not texting at all.

## What are the rules for business texting in the US?

This is the part most articles skip, and it is the part that carries real consequences.

**You need express written consent before sending marketing texts.** Consent for service communication is not the same thing. A customer who gave you their number to book a repair has not consented to promotional messages, and the distinction matters legally.

**Every marketing message needs a clear opt-out**, and opt-outs have to be honored promptly. STOP, UNSUBSCRIBE and the common variants should be processed automatically rather than depending on someone noticing.

**Your business must be registered through [A2P 10DLC](/blog/a2p-10dlc-registration-contractors).** This is the registration US carriers require for businesses texting from a standard 10-digit number. Unregistered traffic gets filtered or blocked outright. Registration involves a brand record, a campaign record and a use-case approval, and carriers process it on their own timeline — typically several business days, sometimes longer.

**Quiet hours matter.** Sending marketing messages late at night is both a compliance issue and a fast way to generate complaints.

None of this is legal advice, and you should confirm your obligations with your own counsel. But it is the setup any competent provider should be handling for you rather than leaving you to discover it after a complaint.

## How do you set up SMS marketing without wrecking your phone number?

The order matters more than people expect.

**Start the A2P 10DLC registration first.** It is the long pole. Everything else can be built while it processes.

**Text-enable the number you already use.** Customers should text the number on your truck. A separate marketing number splits the conversation and confuses people.

**Put replies somewhere a person will see them.** A shared inbox your office can work from, with conversation history. Not a personal cell phone that goes under a house at 9am.

**Build consent capture into the point where you collect numbers.** Booking forms, service agreements, your website. Keep the records.

**Start with transactional messages.** Confirmations, reminders, missed-call replies. These build a clean sending reputation with carriers before you send anything promotional.

**Add promotional messages last, and sparingly.** Seasonal reminders to past customers, tied to a real reason and a real deadline.

## Is SMS marketing worth it for a small contractor?

Usually yes, and often more than for a large one. A two-truck operation misses proportionally more calls than a company with a full-time office manager, which means missed-call text back recovers more.

The honest caveat: if nobody is going to answer the replies, do not start. Texting generates conversations, and an unanswered conversation is worse than silence — the customer now knows you saw them and did not respond. Capacity to answer is the real prerequisite, not budget.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need customer permission to text them?

Yes for marketing messages. You need express written consent, and consent given for service communication does not automatically cover promotional texts. Transactional messages tied to a job the customer booked sit on firmer ground, but the safe approach is capturing explicit consent at the point you collect the number. Confirm your specific obligations with your own counsel.

### What is A2P 10DLC and is it required?

A2P 10DLC is the registration US carriers require for businesses sending texts from a standard 10-digit phone number. It is effectively required — unregistered business traffic gets filtered or blocked, including the appointment confirmations you depend on. Registration covers your brand, your campaign and your use case, and carriers process it on their own schedule.

### How often should I text my customers?

For transactional messages, as often as events warrant — every booking, every reminder, every completed job. For promotional messages, rarely. Seasonal reminders a few times a year tied to a real reason work. Weekly offers train people to opt out.

### Will texting customers hurt my reputation?

Only if the messages are unexpected. A confirmation for an appointment they booked or a reply to a call they made is welcome. An unsolicited promotion to someone who never opted in generates complaints, and those complaints affect both your reputation and your carrier standing.

### What is the difference between SMS marketing and missed call text back?

Missed call text back is one message within a larger system. [SMS marketing](/services/sms-marketing) covers the full set: missed-call replies, confirmations, reminders, en-route notifications, review requests and reactivation campaigns. Most businesses start with missed-call text back because it recovers the most revenue for the least setup, then add the rest.

### Can I use my existing business number for texting?

Yes, and you should. Text-enabling the number already on your trucks and website means customers text the number they already have. A separate marketing number fragments the conversation and looks like a different company.
