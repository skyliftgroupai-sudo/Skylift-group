The reliable way to get more Google reviews is to stop asking in person and start sending an automated text shortly after every completed job, with a direct link to your review page. Asking face to face fails not because customers are unwilling but because your technicians are busy, awkward about it, or simply forget — so the asking happens inconsistently and the reviews arrive inconsistently. A text removes the awkwardness for both sides and lets the customer act immediately, while the job is still fresh.

## Why do contractors struggle to get reviews?

Not because customers are reluctant. Most satisfied homeowners are happy to leave a review if asked at the right moment, in a way that takes thirty seconds.

The failure is operational. Asking in person puts the burden on the technician, at the end of a job, while they are packing up and thinking about the next call. Some techs are comfortable asking. Most are not. The ones who are do not do it every time.

Then there is the promise problem. A customer says "of course, I'll do that tonight" and means it. By evening they are making dinner and it is gone. The intent was real; the follow-through required them to remember, find your listing and type something. Each step loses people.

The result is what most contractors have: a listing with a handful of reviews accumulated over years, most of them clustered around whichever month someone remembered to push for them.

## Why does review consistency matter more than total count?

Because recency is a signal, and because customers read dates.

A homeowner comparing three roofers sees your fifty reviews and notices the most recent is from eighteen months ago. That reads as a business that used to be busy. A competitor with twenty reviews where three arrived this month reads as a business that is currently working.

Recency also matters for [map pack rankings](/services/local-maps), and stale reviews are a common reason [a competitor outranks you](/blog/competitor-ranking-above-me-google-maps). Prominence is one of the factors that decides local ranking, and a steady flow of recent reviews contributes to it in a way that a dormant pile does not.

The practical implication: a system that produces a few reviews every month beats a push that produces twenty and then stops. Consistency is the thing to engineer.

## How does automated review generation work?

The mechanism is simple, which is why it works.

**The job gets marked complete.** In your CRM, your scheduling tool, or by the tech on their phone.

**A text goes out a short time later.** Not immediately — give them a moment after the truck leaves. A short, plain message thanking them and asking if they would leave a review, with a direct link.

**The link goes straight to the review form.** Not to your website, not to your Google listing's homepage. Directly to the form where they can type. Every extra tap loses people.

**Non-responders get one reminder.** A single follow-up a few days later. One, not three.

That is the whole system. What makes it work is not sophistication — it is that it happens every single time without anyone deciding to do it.

## What should the review request actually say?

Short, specific, and human.

Name your company, reference the work that was done, and make the ask directly. "Thanks for having us out today — if you were happy with the water heater install, would you mind leaving a quick review? It genuinely helps us out." Then the link.

What to avoid:

**Do not write a paragraph.** Nobody reads a long text from a business.

**Do not ask for five stars.** Ask for a review. Requesting a specific rating is manipulation, and on some platforms it violates the guidelines.

**Do not send it three days later.** The goodwill fades. Same day or next day is the window.

**Do not send from a number they do not recognize.** It should come from the same business line they have been texting with about the appointment, which is one reason [SMS marketing](/services/sms-marketing) and review requests belong in the same system.

## What should I not do to get reviews?

This section matters more than the rest of the post, because the shortcuts carry real consequences.

**Do not buy reviews.** Google detects review manipulation and the penalties range from removing the reviews to removing the listing. A suspended profile costs more than every review you bought was worth.

**Do not incentivize reviews.** Offering a discount for a review violates Google's policies. It also produces reviews that read as bought, which customers notice.

**Do not gate reviews.** Review gating means asking customers how they felt first and only sending the review link to the happy ones. It is against Google's guidelines. It is also self-defeating — a listing with nothing but perfect reviews reads as fake, and a few honest three-star reviews make the five-star ones credible.

**Do not have staff write reviews.** From their homes, from their phones, from anywhere. This is detectable and it is the fastest route to a suspension.

The uncomfortable truth is that some competitors do these things and appear to get away with it. They get away with it until they do not, and rebuilding a suspended profile costs far more than the shortcut saved.

## Which review platforms are worth the effort?

For a home service business, Google is the one that matters most, and it is not close. It feeds the map pack, it appears in the knowledge panel, and it is where homeowners look. If you only run one review system, point it at Google.

The others are worth knowing about but rarely worth splitting your effort:

**Facebook** matters if a meaningful share of your work comes through local community groups, which in some markets it does. Recommendations there carry weight with a specific audience.

**Yelp** matters in some metro markets and barely registers in others. Check whether it actually ranks for your services in your area before investing in it.

**Trade-specific directories** — Angi, Thumbtack, HomeAdvisor and the rest — are lead sources first and review platforms second. Reviews there help you compete inside that platform, not in Google's results.

**Better Business Bureau** is mostly relevant if your customers are the kind who check it, which skews older.

The mistake to avoid is asking one customer for reviews on four platforms. You will get none. Ask for one, make it Google, and add a second platform only if you have a specific reason.

## What about bad reviews?

You will get some. A steady flow of review requests means occasionally asking someone who was not happy.

This is a feature rather than a bug, for two reasons. First, a perfect record is not believable. Second, a bad review is information — if three people mention the same thing, that is a problem in your business you did not know about.

Respond to them, publicly, without arguing. Acknowledge the specific issue, say what you are doing about it, and offer to sort it out. People reading your listing are judging how you handle problems far more than whether you ever have them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many Google reviews does a contractor need?

There is no threshold number, and anyone quoting one is guessing. What matters more is recency and consistency — a steady flow of recent reviews signals an active business, while a large number from years ago signals the opposite. Aim for a system that produces some every month rather than a target total.

### Is it illegal to pay for Google reviews?

It violates Google's policies regardless of local law, and the consequences are practical rather than legal: reviews removed, listing suspended, ranking lost. Given how hard a suspension is to reverse, the risk is not worth it at any price.

### Can I ask customers to leave a review on a specific platform?

Yes. Asking a customer to review you on Google is fine. What is not fine is asking only the happy ones, or offering something in exchange. Ask everyone, ask for an honest review, offer nothing.

### Should I respond to every review?

Responding to the critical ones matters most, because that is what prospective customers read. Responding to positive ones is worth doing when you can — it is a visible activity signal and it costs a sentence.

### When is the best time to send a review request?

Same day as the job, shortly after your technician leaves, while the customer still feels good about the work. Next day is acceptable. Three days later is too late — the goodwill has faded and the job is no longer front of mind.

### What if a customer leaves a review that is unfair?

Respond calmly and factually, and move on. Google will only remove reviews that violate its policies, which unfair-but-genuine opinions usually do not. A measured public response does more for you than getting the review removed would.
