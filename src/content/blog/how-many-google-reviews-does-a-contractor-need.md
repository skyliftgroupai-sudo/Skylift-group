There is no threshold number of Google reviews, and any specific figure you see quoted is marketing material rather than research. What matters is how you compare to the contractors you are competing against locally, and how recent your reviews are. A business with thirty reviews where several arrived this month typically outperforms one with eighty whose newest is a year old — both in the map pack and with the homeowner reading them. The practical target is not a total; it is a system that produces a few every month without anyone having to remember to ask.

## Why does everyone want a number?

Because a target is easier to act on than a principle, and because plenty of people will happily supply one.

The trouble is that the number depends entirely on context. Fifteen reviews might make you the strongest-reviewed plumber in a small town and leave you invisible in a metro market where competitors have four hundred. The same figure is excellent and useless depending on the postcode.

Google has never published a threshold, and its documentation talks about prominence rather than counts. Anyone telling you "you need fifty" is either generalizing from one market or making it up.

The useful reframe: stop asking how many you need and start asking how you compare to the three businesses currently ranking above you.

## How do I work out my actual target?

Look at who beats you and count theirs.

Search your main service from within your service area, ideally not from your own office — [Google knows where you are](/blog/competitor-ranking-above-me-google-maps) and your own desk gives you a flattering result. Note the three businesses in the map pack.

For each, record two things: total review count and the date of the most recent one. That second column is the one people skip and the one that usually reveals the opportunity.

What you will typically find is one of three situations:

**They have far more and they are current.** A genuine gap. You are playing catch-up on volume and it will take months of consistent requests.

**They have more but they are stale.** The common case, and the best news. Consistent recent reviews can close this faster than the raw totals suggest.

**You have comparable numbers.** Then reviews are not what is holding you back, and you should look at your [primary category and profile setup](/services/local-maps) instead.

That exercise takes fifteen minutes and replaces a guessed target with a real one.

## Does review recency matter more than count?

For ranking, both feed prominence. For the homeowner deciding, recency often matters more.

Put yourself in their position. They are comparing three roofers. One has fifty reviews, newest from fourteen months ago. Another has twenty-five, three from this month. The second reads as a business that is currently working. The first reads as one that used to be.

That impression is doing real work, because a homeowner cannot assess roofing quality. They assess signals, and "still busy" is among the strongest available.

This is also why a review push followed by silence underperforms a steady trickle. Twenty reviews in one month and nothing for a year looks exactly like what it is.

## What about star rating — does perfect matter?

No, and a perfect record can work against you.

A profile with nothing but five stars and no detail reads as curated. Most people have learned to be suspicious of it. A handful of four-star reviews, and the occasional critical one handled well, make the five-star reviews credible.

More importantly, chasing a perfect average pushes businesses toward review gating — asking how someone felt first and only sending the review link to the happy ones. This is against Google's guidelines, and it is self-defeating for the reason above.

What matters far more than the average is how you respond to the bad ones. Prospective customers read those responses closely, because how a contractor handles a problem is more predictive than whether they ever have one.

## How do I get to a useful number?

Make the asking automatic rather than remembered.

The failure is operational, not motivational. Most satisfied customers will leave a review if asked at the right moment in a way that takes thirty seconds. But asking in person puts the burden on a technician who is packing up and thinking about the next job, and the ones who are comfortable asking do not do it every time.

A text shortly after the job completes, with a direct link, removes the awkwardness for both sides and lets the customer act while the work is fresh. One follow-up a few days later for non-responders. That is the whole system, and it works because it happens every time rather than when someone decides to.

The compounding matters: a business completing a few jobs a week and asking every time accumulates steadily, and steadily is exactly the pattern that outperforms bursts. [Automating the request](/services/reviews) is the difference between intending to ask and asking.

## Do reviews mentioning my services and towns help?

Yes, more than most contractors realize, and you have some influence over it without crossing any lines.

Google reads review text. A review saying "replaced our furnace in Whitefish, showed up same day" contains a service, a location and a signal about responsiveness. One saying "great service, thanks" contains nothing. Both are five stars; they are not equally useful.

You cannot tell a customer what to write — that is manipulation and it produces reviews that read as scripted. What you can do is ask in a way that makes specifics likely. A request referencing the actual job — "if you were happy with the water heater install" — prompts people to mention the water heater without being told to.

The same applies to towns. When the request comes after a job in a particular place, customers often name it naturally. Over time that builds a body of review text covering the services and areas you actually work in, which supports [local relevance](/blog/local-seo-home-service-businesses-2026) in a way a pile of generic praise does not.

This is a reason to make review requests job-specific rather than sending an identical template to everyone.

## What should I not do to hit a number?

Everything in this section is faster than the honest route and carries consequences that outlast the gain.

**Buying reviews.** Detectable, and the penalty ranges from removing the reviews to suspending the listing. A suspended profile costs more than every review you bought was worth.

**Incentivizing them.** Offering a discount in exchange violates Google's policies, and the resulting reviews read as bought to anyone paying attention.

**Gating.** Filtering to only ask happy customers. Against the guidelines, and it produces the implausibly perfect profile discussed above.

**Staff reviews.** From their homes, their phones, anywhere. This is among the easiest patterns to detect.

Some competitors do these things and appear to get away with it. They get away with it until they do not, and rebuilding from a suspension takes far longer than the shortcut saved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there a minimum number of reviews to appear in the map pack?

No published minimum exists. Businesses with very few reviews do appear, particularly where competition is thin or the searcher is close by. Reviews contribute to prominence, which is one factor among relevance and distance.

### How many reviews should I aim for each month?

Rather than a fixed figure, aim to ask every customer, every time. A business completing a handful of jobs a week and asking consistently will accumulate at a rate appropriate to its size, which is the right benchmark.

### Do reviews on other platforms help my Google ranking?

Not directly. Google ranks on its own reviews for map pack purposes. Reviews elsewhere help with credibility and can matter within those platforms, but if you are running one review system, point it at Google.

### Will a few bad reviews hurt my rankings?

A handful of critical reviews among many positive ones is normal and does not meaningfully damage you. It can help credibility. What matters is whether you respond, and how.

### How long does it take for reviews to affect rankings?

Reviews contribute to prominence, which moves over months rather than weeks. Consistency compounds — the effect of asking every customer shows up as a trend, not an event.

### Should I ask for reviews from every customer?

Yes, and for an honest review rather than a positive one. Asking selectively is gating, which is against the guidelines. Asking everyone produces a profile that looks like a real business, which is what you want.
