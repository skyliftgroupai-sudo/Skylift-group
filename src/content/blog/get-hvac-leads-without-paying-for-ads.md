The cheapest HVAC leads are the ones you already paid for and never collected: calls that rang out, past customers nobody followed up with, a Google Business Profile that was filled in once, reviews nobody asks for, and a website that does not say where you work. Work those five before you raise an ad budget.

None of this is a secret and none of it is clever. It is unglamorous maintenance work, which is exactly why most HVAC companies skip it and buy clicks instead. The clicks are easier to authorize and easier to measure. They are also the most expensive lead you will ever buy, and they stop the moment the card stops.

## Why does paid advertising feel like the only option?

Because it is the only one with a salesperson attached to it.

Nobody calls an HVAC owner to sell them the idea of returning missed calls. There is no rep whose commission depends on you texting your old customers in October. So the only growth lever anyone ever pitches is the one with an ad platform behind it, and the levers that cost nothing but attention go unpulled for years.

There is a second reason, and it is more honest: the free sources are slower. An ad campaign can produce a call this afternoon. Fixing your Google profile produces calls over the following weeks. When the schedule is thin in a shoulder month, the afternoon option wins, and then it never gets turned off.

The argument for doing the unpaid work is not that ads are bad. It is that paid traffic lands on the same broken foundation. If your phone goes unanswered at 2pm, you are paying for clicks that ring out. Plenty of contractors are [wasting money on Google Ads](/blog/wasting-money-google-ads-contractor) for exactly that reason.

## What is the fastest unpaid source of HVAC leads?

The calls you are already missing. Nothing else comes close, because the intent is already there and you have already paid to create it.

A homeowner with no heat in November is not researching. They are working down a list of search results, and they stop at whoever picks up. If your crew is in an attic and the office is on another line, that call does not go to voicemail — most people do not leave one for a business any more. It goes to the next company.

This is a solved problem. An automatic reply goes out within about a minute of the missed call, asking what they need and offering a time. The caller is usually still holding the phone. They reply instead of dialing the next number, and the job stays with you. We have written about [what a missed call actually costs](/blog/what-missed-calls-cost-contractors) and there is a [calculator](/tools/missed-call-calculator) if you want to put your own numbers to it.

Two weeks of counting is enough to know whether this matters for you. Have whoever answers the phone keep a tally of calls that rang out. Multiply by your average job value and by a conservative guess at how many of those callers would have booked. Most HVAC owners are surprised by the figure, and the surprise is usually in one direction.

## How much work is sitting in your existing customer list?

More than in any ad account you could open this week.

An HVAC company that has been trading for five years has hundreds or thousands of people in its records who have already paid it money, already let its technicians into their homes, and already decided it does acceptable work. That is the hardest part of selling, and it is done. Most of those people have not heard from the company since the invoice.

The seasonal structure of the trade makes this worse than it is in other trades. A furnace tune-up is an annual event that almost nobody remembers to book. A system installed eight years ago is approaching the age where a replacement conversation is reasonable. A customer who had a capacitor replaced in July is a candidate for a maintenance plan. None of these people will call you unprompted. All of them will respond to a reminder.

The practical route is [texting the customers you already have](/services/sms-marketing) rather than emailing them. Email from a contractor goes to the promotions tab. A text about a furnace tune-up before the first cold snap arrives on the device in their hand and gets read. There are rules attached — you need consent, an opt-out on every message, and carrier registration through A2P 10DLC — and we cover those in the guide to [registration and compliance](/blog/a2p-10dlc-registration-contractors). Do not skip them; the penalties are real and the carriers filter aggressively.

The same list is the basis for a [reactivation campaign](/services/database-reactivation): a focused run at people who have not booked in a year or more. It is the cheapest work most HVAC companies will book all year, and there is [more on how to structure it](/blog/get-past-customers-to-book-again) if you want the detail.

## Does a Google Business Profile actually bring in HVAC calls?

Yes, and for local emergency work it is frequently the first thing a homeowner sees — above the website, above the ads.

The map results answer a specific kind of question: who is near me, open, and apparently competent. A profile that has the right primary category, real service areas, current hours, photographs of actual work, and a steady flow of reviews answers that question better than one filled in during setup and never touched.

Most of the gap between HVAC companies in the map pack is not mysterious. It is categories set wrong, service areas left empty, hours that do not reflect the emergency line, and no posts or photos for two years. Working through [profile optimization](/services/local-maps) is a weekend of admin that keeps paying, and we have written a longer piece on [why a competitor might be ranking above you](/blog/competitor-ranking-above-me-google-maps).

It is worth being clear that this is slower than the phone fix. You will not see the effect for weeks, and proximity to the searcher is a factor you cannot change. It still belongs on the list because the cost is your time, not your budget.

## How do reviews turn into booked jobs?

By deciding which of the three companies in the map pack gets called first.

A homeowner comparing HVAC contractors on their phone at 8am is not reading review text closely. They are scanning the star rating and the number next to it, and recency matters — a business whose last review is from two years ago reads as one that may not still be trading.

The failure here is almost never quality of work. It is that nobody asks. A technician who has just finished a job intends to mention it, gets pulled to the next call, and does not. The fix is to take it out of anyone's memory: the request goes automatically after the job is marked complete, by text, with a direct link. That is what [automating the request](/services/reviews) means in practice, and we have covered [how many reviews a contractor actually needs](/blog/how-many-google-reviews-does-a-contractor-need) separately.

## Does the website need to be big?

No. It needs to say where you work, what you fix, and how to reach you, and it needs to load on a phone in a driveway.

Most HVAC websites fail on specificity rather than size. A page that says "serving the greater metro area" tells neither a homeowner nor a search engine anything. A page that names the towns you actually cover, the systems you actually service, and the emergency hours you actually keep is more useful to both, and it is a couple of hours of writing rather than a rebuild.

The other common failure is the phone number. It should be tappable, above the fold, on every page, and it should be the number that someone answers. The rest of the site matters far less than contractors think.

## Which of these should an HVAC company do first?

In this order, for most companies:

1. **Stop losing calls.** Highest value, fastest payback, and it makes everything else you spend money on work better.
2. **Text your existing customers.** The list is already there. Start with seasonal maintenance reminders, which are the least intrusive and the easiest to justify.
3. **Fix the Google profile.** A weekend of admin, then a monthly habit of posts and photos.
4. **Automate the review request.** One-time setup, compounds quietly.
5. **Tighten the website copy.** Name the towns, name the systems, make the number tappable.

Doing the first two will change more about your booked schedule than the other three combined, which is inconvenient, because they are the two that feel least like marketing.

## What does this not replace?

Paid advertising, when you want volume on demand.

Everything above is durable and cheap and slow to build. None of it produces fifty extra calls next Tuesday because you decided you wanted them. If you have capacity to fill on a specific date, or you are entering a market where nobody has heard of you, ads do something the unpaid channels cannot.

The argument is about sequence, not about virtue. Fix the leaks first, then turn on the tap. An HVAC company that answers every call, texts its own list, and shows up properly in the map pack gets more out of the same ad budget than one that does not — and it keeps a base of work if the budget ever has to go to zero.
