For most home service businesses, missed call text back converts better than an answering service, because it starts a conversation the customer can answer immediately instead of promising a callback they have to wait for. An answering service takes a message and hands you a callback task; by the time you make that call, the urgent customer has usually booked a competitor. The answering service wins in one specific situation: when callers genuinely need to speak to a person and will not engage by text, which is more common with older customer bases and complex commercial work.

## What does each one actually do?

**An answering service** routes your unanswered calls to a human operator, usually offsite. They answer in your business name, take the caller's details and the nature of the problem, and send you a message. Some can book into a calendar if you give them access. You typically pay per call or per minute.

**Missed call text back** detects the unanswered call and automatically sends the caller a text within about a minute — your business name, an acknowledgment, and a question about what they need. The caller replies by text, and the conversation lands in a shared inbox your office works from. You typically pay a flat monthly rate.

The structural difference: one produces a message for you to action, the other produces a conversation already in progress.

## Which one gets more customers booked?

The text back, in most cases, and the reason is timing rather than technology.

A homeowner with an urgent problem is working down search results. They call you, you do not answer, and they are already dialing the next company. The window where you can still win that job is measured in minutes.

An answering service does not close that window. The operator takes a message, you get notified, and then someone has to call back. Even a fast callback often arrives after the customer has spoken to a competitor who answered live. The customer's problem was not "nobody took my message" — it was "nobody could help me right now."

A text back lands inside the window. It arrives while they are still deciding, it names your company, and it asks a question they can answer with one thumb while doing something else. Plenty of people find that easier than taking a call, especially at work.

There is a second effect worth noting: a text creates a written thread. The address, the problem and the agreed time are all recorded. Answering services introduce a transcription step, and details get garbled in transcription more often than anyone admits.

## Where does an answering service actually win?

Being fair about this matters, because the text back is not universally better.

**Callers who will not text.** Some customer bases skew heavily toward wanting a person. If your work is with older homeowners or in a market where texting a business feels odd, a live voice converts better.

**Genuinely complex intake.** Commercial work, multi-property management, or anything where the first conversation involves questions a text thread handles badly.

**True emergencies needing judgment.** Gas smell, water near an electrical panel, structural damage. These need a human to triage and escalate, not a booking flow. A good answering service handles this well.

**Businesses with nobody to work the inbox.** A text back generates replies, and replies need answering. If there is genuinely nobody to do that within an hour, an answering service that handles the whole interaction may fit better.

That last one is the honest constraint. Text back shifts work rather than eliminating it — from "be available at the exact second the phone rings" to "work through replies within the hour." That is a much easier problem, but it is not no problem.

## How do the costs compare?

The pricing models differ in a way that matters most when you are busiest.

Answering services generally bill per call or per minute. That means your costs rise exactly when call volume spikes — during a heat wave, after a storm, when your marketing is working. The channel gets more expensive precisely when you are getting the most value from it, which is a strange incentive.

Text back is typically a flat monthly cost regardless of volume. A quiet month and a record month cost the same.

For a business with low, steady call volume, the answering service can work out cheaper. For anything seasonal — which is most of the trades — the flat model usually wins, and it wins hardest in the months that matter most.

## Can I use both?

Yes, and for some businesses it is the right answer.

A common split: text back handles everything automatically, and calls flagged as emergencies route to a live answering service or an on-call technician. The automation covers volume, the human covers judgment.

Another split by time: live answering during business hours when someone can pick up anyway, automated text back for evenings and weekends when nobody is there.

An [AI voice agent](/services/ai-voice-agents) sits between the two — it answers the call in a natural voice, qualifies the job, checks real calendar availability and books the appointment during the conversation. The caller hangs up with a confirmed time rather than a promise, which is the thing an answering service cannot do without calendar access and the thing a text back does more slowly.

## What should a contractor choose?

A rough decision path:

**Start with text back if** your work is urgent-driven, your customers are comfortable texting, your call volume is seasonal, and someone can work an inbox within the hour. That covers most residential HVAC, plumbing, roofing and junk removal businesses.

**Choose an answering service if** your callers strongly prefer voice, your intake is complex, or nobody can reliably respond to text replies.

**Use both if** you have a real emergency category that needs human triage, or you want live coverage in hours and automation outside them.

**Fix this before scaling ads either way.** Paying for a click and then missing the resulting call is the most expensive mistake available. Response handling should be in place before [Google Ads](/services/google-ads) spend goes up, not after.

## What about the calls that are not customers?

A detail that rarely makes the comparison and matters in practice.

A meaningful share of inbound calls to any contractor are not jobs — sales calls, suppliers, wrong numbers, people looking for a service you do not offer. With an answering service you pay for every one of those, because billing is per call. The operator takes a message from a software vendor and you are charged for it.

Automated text back filters them for free. Sales callers do not reply to a text asking what service they need. Wrong numbers do not reply. Suppliers call back directly or email. The people who reply are, almost by definition, the people who wanted to hire you.

That changes the cost comparison in a way the headline pricing hides. You are not comparing cost per call; you are comparing cost per call that could have been a job. Ask any answering service what share of your calls they handled turned out to be non-customers, and factor that in before comparing the monthly figures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How fast does missed call text back respond?

Within about a minute. That speed is the whole mechanism — the reply has to land while the caller is still working down search results, not after they have booked someone else.

### Do customers actually reply to automated texts?

Yes, when the message names your business and refers to the call they just made. It reads as responsive rather than automated. What does not work is a generic message with no context, or one from a number they do not recognize.

### Is an answering service better for emergencies?

For genuine emergencies needing judgment — gas, water near electrics, structural issues — a human is better, whether that is an answering service or an on-call tech. Many businesses route flagged emergencies to a person and automate everything else.

### What happens if nobody answers the text replies?

You have made things worse. The customer now knows you saw them and did not respond. Capacity to work the inbox is the real prerequisite for text back, more than budget.

### Can an answering service book appointments?

Some can, if you give them calendar access. It is worth asking, because a service that only takes messages leaves the customer waiting for a callback, which is the weakness that loses urgent jobs.

### Which is cheaper for a small contractor?

It depends on volume. Answering services bill per call, so low steady volume can be cheaper. Text back is usually flat, which wins during seasonal spikes — and spikes are when you miss the most calls. Work it out against your own busiest month, not your average one.
